Abstract
Objective.Determine how many forearm surface electromyography electrodes can be removed
while preserving mean gesture recognition for unseen participants, and assess the additional
value of attribution-based selection.Approach.We analyzed 43 GRABMyo participants
using sixteen monopolar electrodes and sixteen active gestures. Participant-disjoint inner
cross-fitting estimated TreeSHAP rankings within each outer leave-one-subject-out fold.
Reduced models were retrained and compared with full16 using participant-bootstrap
intervals and a predefined 0.02 Macro-F1 engineering tolerance. Exploratory analyses
included primary-budget geometric selection, paired gesture predictions, shared-resource
permutation selection, and task, window and classifier sensitivities.Main results.SHAP12
achieved mean Macro-F1 0.528 versus 0.541 for full16; its change was -0.0133 (95% CI
-0.0160 to -0.0106). The smallest qualifying count was 12. Nevertheless, 11 participants lost
more than 0.02 and 4 gestures showed corrected declines. Geometric12 did not establish
non-inferiority, and the direct SHAP12 mean advantage remained uncertain (+0.0060; 95%
CI -0.0010 to +0.0127). Attribution outperformed mean random subsets with the
reduced-budget classifier. Shared-resource SHAP outperformed grouped permutation at six
electrodes, but its twelve-electrode advantage remained uncertain. Stability regularization
did not establish an improvement.Significance.One quarter of this monopolar array could
be removed within the mean tolerance under offline participant-independent evaluation.
This validates a selection procedure rather than one locked physical layout; individual losses
and uncertain transfer require independent validation before device claims.
1 Introduction
Surface electromyography (sEMG) provides a non-invasive signal for gesture recognition and my-
oelectric control, but the recording interface must balance spatial information against the burden
of additional electrodes (Scheme and Englehart, 2011; Farina et al., 2014; Vujaklija et al., 2016).
Fewer recording sites could simplify an interface. Whether that reduction preserves recognition for an
unseen participant is an empirical question, and offline recognition alone does not establish a usable
control interface.
Channel selection has been studied with feature filters, wrappers and spatial criteria (Phinyomark
et al., 2012; Stango et al., 2015; Qu et al., 2021). SHAP-guided EMG channel and feature optimization
is also established (Rani et al., 2025); related work uses Shapley explanations for similar sEMG
gestures (Wang et al., 2024) and explainable feature selection with fusion (Gehlot et al., 2024). These
contributions make attribution-based reduction a relevant comparator rather than a new algorithmic
premise. Their performance estimates cannot be directly compared with this study across different
tasks, sensors and validation protocols.
For a system intended to work across people, selection must exclude the eventual test participant.
Using that participant to standardize features, estimate importance or choose a subset can produce
optimistic estimates. Participant-disjoint inner evaluation also avoids measuring attribution solely
on observations used to fit the attributing model. This motivates an outer leave-one-subject-out
(LOSO) evaluation in which each entire selection procedure is reconstructed from the outer training
participants.

Attribution magnitude describes predictive reliance of a fitted model, not a direct measurement of
muscle activation. TreeSHAP distributes a model’s prediction over its input features (Lundberg and
Lee, 2017; Lundberg et al., 2020); aggregation over an electrode’s features offers a ranking, but does
not turn it into a coalition-level Shapley value for that electrode. Correlated features and variation
across participants can affect this ranking. Attribution stability has already motivated resampling
and regularization methods (Visani et al., 2022; Xiang et al., 2026); a stability penalty therefore
requires validation rather than a novelty claim.
Electrode count requires an explicit tolerance for performance loss. Non-inferiority asks whether
a reduced system remains within such a tolerance, whereas a non-significant superiority test does
not establish preservation (Schuirmann, 1987; Walker and Nowacki, 2011). A mean tolerance is also
different from preserving every participant or gesture. Reporting only an aggregate curve could hide
losses that matter to an intended application.
We evaluate cross-fitted electrode reduction on GRABMyo and separately examine NinaPro DB5.
The primary question is the minimum count supported by a specified engineering margin. Secondary
questions concern utility beyond random and spatial selection, stability regularization, and deletion
with retraining. Exploratory analyses use a geometric subset under the primary training protocol,
paired participant and gesture predictions, and shared-resource SHAP and grouped permutation
rankings. Sensitivities and split-derived layouts delimit the claim: the LOSO experiment evaluates a
selection procedure, not a prospectively validated common physical layout.
2 Methods
2.1 Datasets, selection units and signal processing
GRABMyo v1.1.0 contains 43 able-bodied participants recorded over 3 days with an EMGUSB2+
amplifier at 2048 Hz (Pradhan et al., 2022; Jiang et al., 2024). Source-reported demographics were
23 male and 20 female, age 26.4 ± 2.9 years. The forearm montage has sixteen monopolar sites
in two rings (figure 1); each site supplies one channel, so electrode and channel selection coincide
here. Counts refer only to these signal recording sites; reference and ground requirements are not
evaluated. The primary task uses sixteen active gestures, excluding rest. All 30,715 distributed files
matched published checksums, and 15,351 records were processed. Source demographics and the
official gesture dictionary are provided with the reproducibility package.
Signals were filtered at 20–450 Hz using a Butterworth band-pass with SciPy order argument 4
(eight poles per pass), applied forward and backward for zero phase, followed by a forward–backward
50 Hz notch ( Q = 30). They were then divided into 250 ms windows with 50% overlap within trial
boundaries. The non-causal filter limits the analysis to offline use. Each channel supplied mean
absolute value, RMS, variance, waveform length, zero crossings, slope sign changes, integrated EMG,
Willison amplitude, mean frequency and median frequency. The active-task cache contains 563,472
windows with 160 features each.
Count-feature thresholds followed a common rule: 1.15 times the median absolute signal first
difference, rounded to one significant figure. The monopolar threshold (10 −2 mV) was calibrated from
54 unfiltered records from six participants before participant partitioning. Excluding each participant
left the rounded threshold unchanged in all 43 folds. Threshold calibration therefore involved pooled
development data, although its value was invariant to participant exclusion. Fold-specific feature
means and standard deviations, classifier fitting and attribution ranking exclude the outer test
participant.
NinaPro DB5 was analyzed separately: 10 participants, two Myo armbands, sixteen channels
sampled at 200 Hz and exercise 1’s twelve finger movements (Pizzolato et al., 2017). Refined labels
determine movement segments. Filtering used an effective passband of 10–89 Hz without a notch. The
250 ms windows contain 50 samples. The threshold is seven quantization steps in the stored integer
representation; every participant-exclusion check reproduced it. Source-reported DB5 demographics
were 8 male and 2 female, age 28.0 ± 4.0 years. Sex is reported as recorded in the source datasets; no
sex-specific effect is estimated. No anatomical correspondence between DB5 and GRABMyo channel
numbers is assumed.
EthicsThis is a secondary analysis of publicly distributed, de-identified recordings, with no
new recruitment or recording. Original study approvals and consent are described in the dataset
publications.

Pooling people within five inner folds can suppress person-level heterogeneity. A post-hoc extension

outer-training participants. These vectors share five fitted inner models; their dispersion is descriptive
heterogeneity, not a standard error. All penalties and counts are evaluated under the primary protocol.
A post-hoc descriptive analysis compared selected physical site sets and Jaccard similarity at every
fixed penalty and count. It assesses whether penalties change the physical inputs and does not select
a penalty.
2.4 Primary endpoint and participant-level inference
Macro-F1 is the unweighted mean of the gesture F1 scores for each held-out participant. For count k,
the paired change is ∆ p,k = F1p,k −F1 p,16. The predefined primary engineering tolerance is δ = 0.02

on the 0–1 scale. The primary decision rule requires the lower 95% percentile-bootstrap bound on the
participant mean change to exceed −δ; k∗ is the smallest qualifying count below sixteen. Sensitivity
margins are 0.01 and 0.03. This criterion concerns mean recognition performance, has no established
clinical interpretation and does not imply equivalence or individual preservation.
The participant is the statistical unit: 43 for GRABMyo, ten for DB5 and 21 or 22 test participants
for split-layout checks. Bootstrap intervals use 10,000 resamples of paired participant scores. They
are conditional on the observed fitted LOSO models. Overlapping training cohorts and the lack
of refitting within every bootstrap mean these intervals do not capture all new-cohort training
uncertainty (Bengio and Grandvalet, 2004).
The percentile-tail Holm procedure uses the fraction of unshifted bootstrap mean differences at
or below −δ, with a Monte Carlo floor. This is a percentile-tail quantity rather than a bootstrap
distribution generated under the null. An exploratory sensitivity uses a null-centred studentized
bootstrap (Davison and Hinkley, 1997). For dp = ∆ p,k, residuals dp − ¯d are resampled, their
studentized means are compared with ( ¯d + δ)/(sd/√n), and the upper-tail probability uses a plus-one
correction. Holm adjustment covers the fifteen reduced-count tests at one-sided α = 0.025, separately
for each condition, margin and penalty.
Paired secondary contrasts use Wilcoxon signed-rank tests and participant mean-change percentile
intervals. These address different estimands: signed ranks are not a test of the mean. Holm families
are stated explicitly; a non-significant contrast is not evidence of equivalence. The secondary test
families are post-hoc and are not prospectively confirmatory.
2.5 Selection comparators and attribution diagnostics
Reduced-budget comparisons use random subsets, cross-fitted SHAP, stability-regularized SHAP
(λ = 0 .5), feature-wise permutation, mutual information and geometric farthest-point selection.
Random selection uses 100 subsets per participant at counts 4, 6 and 8, and 500 at count 12. Eight
attribution-versus-mean-random contrasts receive Holm correction. Feature-wise permutation uses one
model fitted on 20,000 windows and three feature-wise permutations on 8,000 non-overlapping windows
from the same outer-training participant pool. Its 40,000-window ranking pool was standardized
before the window split; this was not participant-disjoint inner validation. The outer test participant
remained excluded. SHAP instead uses the participant-disjoint cross-fitting above. Thus shared final
evaluation does not equalize their selection resources or validation designs.
Exploratory geometric selection uses the primary 300-round, 200,000-window protocol, the same
outer training sample and full16 reference. Geometric selection uses the fixed two-ring topology,
nominal 24 cm circumference and 2 cm separation, starts at F1 and successively maximizes distance
from selected sites; ties favor the lower index. It is not tuned to participant outcomes. The complete
geometric count curve evaluates this ordering under the primary protocol. It is a post-hoc analysis
motivated by the twelve-electrode comparison.
A separate exploratory resource-matched comparison shares five inner models between SHAP and
grouped-electrode permutation, with up to 200,000 uniformly sampled training and 2,000 validation
windows per fold. Equal validation quotas represent every inner-held-out participant. All ten features
of one electrode are permuted jointly within each participant, three repeats, scoring the equally
weighted mean participant Macro-F1 loss. Signed losses are retained, normalized by their summed
absolute magnitude, and aggregated by the median across inner folds. SHAP uses the identical
observations and normalizes absolute contribution magnitudes. Final models at counts 6 and 12
use the primary protocol; two contrasts receive Holm correction. Measured training and selection
times are reported separately. Runtime measurements include concurrent workstation load and are
descriptive rather than an isolated benchmark. Because permutation grouping, participant-disjoint
validation, scaler isolation and resources all differ from feature-wise permutation, this experiment
does not isolate a budget effect.
Deletion with retraining uses the unpenalized primary ordering and compares removal of high-
ranked, random and low-ranked electrodes using the lighter model. Its fifteen contrasts form one
Holm family. This diagnostic tests predictive ranking relevance; it cannot identify causal muscles or
validate an online controller.
2.6 Participant and gesture degradation
Primary full16, SHAP12 and geometry12 predictions are indexed to identical outer-test observations.
Participant losses, quantiles, worst loss and the number exceeding the primary margin are descriptive.
Gesture-specific F1 differences are paired over all participants, with Holm correction across sixteen
gestures. Confusion changes average row-normalized confusion matrices within participants so people,

2.7 Sensitivity, transfer and fixed-layout scope
Rest-inclusive recognition and 150/500 ms windows use raw-signal re-extraction and complete count
curves with the primary 250 ms ranking held fixed. The 150 ms window is 307 samples with stride
153; 250/500 ms use 512/1024 samples and half-window strides. The same 200,000-window count
does not equate recording duration across windows. A random-forest sensitivity uses a 200-tree,
minimum-leaf-size-2 configuration at counts 12 and 16, the unchanged XGBoost ranking and the same
training sample. It tests those counts only and cannot establish a random-forest minimum count.
DB5 applies the procedure separately with its own sixteen-channel reference. Derived bipolar
signals Bj = Fj −F j+8 use eight aligned pairs, re-extracted features and the eight-pair reference;
they are not physical bipolar-sensor measurements. Full-array cross-session analyses compare training
on session 1 with testing on sessions 1–3, and training on sessions 1–2 with testing on session 3. They
do not test a reduced layout under displacement, and the latter comparison confounds data quantity
with session diversity.
Fixed-layout checks use odd-ID development/even-ID testing and the reverse, each deriving a
layout using development participants only and the five-fold median/MAD aggregation rule. Their
inner and final models use all development windows with training-only standardization and the
same 300-round classifier, rather than the primary curve’s 200,000-window cap. The pooled feature
thresholds also apply to these checks. The count of twelve had already been selected using the
full cohort, so neither direction independently validates count selection. The layouts differ and
must be reported separately. A post-hoc Monte Carlo sensitivity analysis repeated the percentile
bootstrap under 200 fixed randomizations, with 10,000 resamples per repetition. The distribution
of decisions quantifies numerical sensitivity near the boundary; it does not estimate the probability
that non-inferiority is true. No fresh, independently collected cohort with this montage was available.
Feature redundancy and ranking-agreement diagnostics are documented in the Supplementary; high
outer-fold agreement is qualified by extensive training-set overlap.
3 Results
3.1 How many electrodes meet the mean performance tolerance?
Under the primary curve protocol, full16 achieved mean Macro-F1 0.541 and SHAP12 achieved 0.528.
The smallest count meeting the 0.02 margin was 12, with paired change -0.0133 (95% CI -0.0160
to -0.0106). Eleven did not meet this criterion: change -0.0204 (-0.0246 to -0.0162). The primary
percentile decision and the exploratory null-centred, Holm-adjusted sensitivity gave the same primary
minimum count: 14, 12 and 11 at margins 0.01, 0.02 and 0.03, respectively. The four penalty curves
did not change these count decisions (figure 3; full results in the Supplementary).
Mean preservation did not extend uniformly to individuals. For SHAP12 minus full16, the median
change was -0.0125 and participant SD was +0.0093. 1 participant improved, while 11 lost more than
0.02. The worst observed change was -0.0361 (participant 27), and the best was +0.0144. The margin
is a mean-level engineering criterion, so these counts do not redefine individual non-inferiority.

performance was not used to choose one.
At twelve electrodes, every fixed participant-level penalty selected the same physical site set as its
unpenalized counterpart in all 43 outer folds. These identical inputs give identical scores under the
fixed classifier protocol; this is not an equivalence test of different layouts.
3.4 Where does the electrode-count conclusion stop generalizing?
The two split-derived fixed layouts gave different results. Odd-ID development/even-ID testing
removed 3, 9, 11, 12, with change -0.0124 (-0.0179 to -0.0077), meeting the 0.02 criterion. The reverse
removed 9, 11, 12, 13, with change -0.0135 (-0.020020 to -0.0066), whose lower bound missed the
criterion. The reverse split met the percentile criterion in 90 of 200 Monte Carlo repetitions (45%),
indicating numerical decision fragility rather than a probability that non-inferiority is true. These are
two layouts rather than one layout validated twice; the count had already been chosen using the full
cohort. Neither is an independent prospective validation.

4 Discussion
The study supports removing one quarter of the GRABMyo monopolar recording sites within a
defined mean recognition tolerance under this offline LOSO protocol. It does not show that every
participant or gesture is preserved. The observed individual losses and corrected gesture declines
make this distinction practically relevant: count selection for a real interface should include task
priorities and user-specific failure criteria rather than relying solely on a pooled mean.
Attribution supplied predictive information beyond the mean random-subset baseline, and deletion
with retraining supported its ranking relevance. Spatial coverage was a more competitive comparator.
The matched primary experiment places geometric12’s mean loss near the tolerance boundary; the
direct mean advantage of SHAP12 remains uncertain. The full geometric curve required 15 retained
sites under the primary margin and decision rule, but only one deterministic geometric ordering
was tested. These results motivate attribution as one evaluated selection procedure, with additional
computational cost, rather than establishing a universal advantage over spatial selection. The
shared-model experiment found a positive SHAP mean advantage over grouped permutation at six
retained electrodes, but no established mean advantage at twelve. The gain under more aggressive
reduction does not mean that six electrodes met the primary tolerance. It changes permutation
grouping, participant-disjoint validation, scaler isolation and resources together, so it cannot attribute
differences solely to computational budget.
Stability regularization was not an established improvement. Five-fold pooling obscures some
person heterogeneity, which motivated the participant-level extension, but more descriptive dispersion
does not imply that subtracting it improves classification. All penalty settings were fixed and
disclosed rather than selected on outer scores. Correlated electrode features, normalization and shared
attributing models complicate physiological interpretation; high attribution is model reliance and can
reflect redundancy, task discrimination or montage geometry without identifying a particular muscle.
The inference has two distinct limits. First, the tolerance is an engineering choice, not a clinically
validated equivalence bound; tighter or looser tolerances support different counts. Second, participant
bootstrap intervals are conditional on fitted models with strongly overlapping training sets. The
null-centred sensitivity corrects how the bootstrap test distribution is constructed, but cannot remove
training-set dependence or development exposure. A nested development cohort, independently
locked tolerance and external validation would strengthen future inference.

Several aspects restrict transfer. Only able-bodied public datasets were analyzed, not target
clinical users. Zero-phase filtering is non-causal. Complete rest/window curves retain the primary
ranking; the classifier sensitivity also retains it and samples only two counts. DB5 uses different
sensors, sampling, labels and a small cohort, and derived bipolar pairs are constructed signals. The
two split layouts differ and count selection had already seen the cohort. Full-array cross-session
results do not demonstrate reduced-layout displacement robustness. No real-time accuracy, latency,
energy, hardware cost reduction or clinical benefit was measured. Clinical channel-count experiments
provide a further reason to keep this scope narrow: real-time prosthesis control can benefit from
additional channels even when offline reduction appears promising (Simon et al., 2024). Such evidence
uses a different cohort, interface and endpoint and cannot validate or refute the present offline mean
tolerance.
A prospective follow-up should fix a single geometry and count using development data only,
record new participants with the documented montage, and define participant- and gesture-level
loss criteria before testing. It should evaluate causal preprocessing and a closed-loop task, recording
prediction latency and electrode failures as measured outcomes.
5 Conclusion
Cross-fitted attribution supported reduction of the GRABMyo monopolar array to 12 electrodes at
a 0.02 mean Macro-F1 tolerance under participant-independent offline evaluation. Utility beyond
random selection was supported. Under shared resources, SHAP exceeded grouped permutation at
six electrodes; its twelve-electrode mean advantage remained uncertain, as did the primary SHAP12–
geometry12 contrast. Stability regularization did not establish an improvement. Participant losses,
gesture-specific degradation, distinct fixed layouts and representation-dependent results restrict the
claim to an evaluated reduction procedure. Independent validation is still required for a locked
physical interface.
